> Grammar is a set of conventions sedimented from frequent use, not a pre-installed system; in class it emerges from interaction, and the teacher's job is largely reactive.

## Claim
Language emerges on three timescales: historical (pidgins → creoles; *going to* → *gonna*), ontogenetic (each learner's acquisition) and microgenetic (moment-to-moment classroom talk). Grammar = routinised patterns abstracted from frequent usage. Pedagogically: learners lead, the teacher scaffolds and recasts learner-initiated episodes.

## Mechanism / theory
- Usage-based / emergentist SLA: N. Ellis (1998, *Language Learning* 48/4); Ellis, Römer & O'Donnell (2016) — connectionist modelling; Hopper (1998) "emergent grammar"; Holland (1998) complex adaptive systems; Christiansen & Chater (2016) *Creating Language* — the "forest track" metaphor (repeated use wears paths).
- Contrasted with innatism (Pinker 1994).
- Breen (1985): classroom language is "a communal product derived through a jointly constructed process".

## Classroom moves
- Expect and *log* emergent language rather than trying to pre-specify all of it.
- Scaffold and recast what learners try to say; let them initiate.
- Recycle items across lessons so repeated use "lays down tracks".
- Teacher as guide pointing out landmarks, not pre-defining the route.

## Limits & critique
- Geoff Jordan (comment): Dogme/TBLT don't *need* emergentism — Breen and Long predate it; there are two emergentisms (Ellis's input-driven vs O'Grady's processor-driven). O'Grady (2010): "no comprehensive emergentist theory of language" yet.
- The post doesn't engage the meta-analytic evidence for explicit instruction (e.g. Norris & Ortega 2000). *(Claude)*
