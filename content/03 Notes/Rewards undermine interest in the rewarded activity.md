> 'Do this and you'll get that' reduces interest in the activity. Performance-contingent rewards do most damage. 'The best that carrots — or sticks — can do is change people's behavior temporarily.'

## Evidence cited (NYT, 2018)
- Pittman et al. (2008): rewards for finishing early → no help or more procrastination.
- HKU/NYU (2015): attendance rewards for 9-year-olds in India → a brief rise, then attendance fell.
- Robinson et al. (Harvard working paper, 2018): >15,000 students in 14 California districts; attendance rewards had no effect or made it worse.
- Gubler et al. (2015): attendance rewards at a laundry company → little effect, some workers came less.
- Framing: "rewards, like punishments, are ultimately about power".

## Counter-evidence *(Claude)*
- **Cameron & Pierce (1994)** meta-analysis: overall effects of rewards on intrinsic motivation were small. Verbal rewards increased it.
- **Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999)**, 128 studies: *expected tangible* rewards (engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent) reduced free-choice intrinsic motivation. *Unexpected* rewards and *positive feedback* didn't, and verbal feedback increased it.
- So Kohn's claim is stronger than the evidence, but the core mechanism (expected, tangible, contingent rewards for already-interesting tasks) is well supported.
