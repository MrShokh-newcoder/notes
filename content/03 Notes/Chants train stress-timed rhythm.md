> A chant is a short, memorable phrase with exaggerated but natural stress, rhythm and intonation. Unlike a song it has no tune distorting speech, so it trains English's stress-timed rhythm, until it becomes a monotonous drawl.

## Doing it well
- Exaggerate stress on content words and let function words shrink ("They're PLAYing FOOTball").
- Count the group in with gestures; train children to follow you like a conductor.
- Vary speed and volume; stop *before* it becomes a drawl.
- Join in yourself; children won't risk looking silly unless you do.

## Building chants ([source](https://jmhdr.wordpress.com/2020/05/18/where-to-start-when-creating-chants/))
- **Story chant (AB AB AB C)**: lift a line, repeat it three times, add a closing line ("I shouldn't have done it. I really shouldn't.") Used with 11–12-year-olds.
- **Vocabulary chant** (from Caroline Graham): sort target words by syllable count (2/3/1), combine one from each into a 4/4 beat: "apples, oranges, pears". Ages 6–7+.
- Similar-sounding words make tongue twisters ("carrots, cauliflower, corn"). Students then write and teach their own.

## Theory & limits
Stress- vs syllable-timing (contested in phonetics research: English isn't strictly isochronous *(Claude)*); Lenneberg via Aitchison on rhythm and memory; Graham's *Jazz Chants*. No outcome evidence.
