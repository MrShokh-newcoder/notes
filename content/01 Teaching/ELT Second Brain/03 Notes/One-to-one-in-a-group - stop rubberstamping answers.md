> In whole-class mode, 'press pause', work intensively on one learner's utterance with minimal hints until it's upgraded, in full view of the group, then release. Don't rubberstamp: 'to learn anything must involve effort and a degree of discomfort or struggle'.

## Procedure ([PDF](https://demandhighelt.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/01/one-to-one-in-a-group-jim.pdf))
1. **Don't rubberstamp.** Withhold the automatic "Very good". Process the answer, then decide to validate or push. (Practitioners report: ask the class "Is Sally right?")
2. **Select** one upgradeable utterance that helps others too.
3. **Establish and decide**: the learner repeats it; choose *one* feature to work on.
4. **Hint with "the least that is enough"**: a gesture, an echo with rising intonation, a finger per word.
5. **Nudge and reflect**: "Say it faster", "Change the second word"; then ask "What did you change?"
6. **Bring others in, release**: eye contact with the class; work through the first point of interest (1POI), then the next (2POI); end on a clear high.

## Limits
Scrivener admits there's "no research data" and that it "sounds horrendously complicated". A DELTA candidate found the lesson ran long (over an hour for 8 questions).

## Apply in my context *(Claude)*
- This is Lyster's *prompts* turned into a routine (see [[Error correction - types of error and a response sequence]]). It's very effective for IELTS speaking groups of 8–12; with 25 teens, limit it to 1–2 episodes per lesson.
- Not rubberstamping also fits Kohn's critique of "Good job!" ([[Five reasons to stop saying Good job]]).
