> Vocabulary learning is slow and limited by working memory. Fewer items, taught deeper, recycled more often. Chunking doesn't raise capacity, but it makes each item more functional.

## Conti's targets (his synthesis, not study findings)
| Group | New items per lesson |
|---|---|
| Year 3 (age 7–8) | 2–3 |
| Year 4 | 3–4 |
| Year 5 | 4–5 |
| Year 6 (age 10–11) | 5 (occasionally 6) |
| Secondary | 6–10 (listening-heavy lessons at the low end) |

"Chunking does not allow us to teach more words — it allows us to teach language more effectively."

## Mechanism / theory
Working memory (Miller 1956; Cowan 2001; Gathercole & Alloway 2008); chunking (Wray 2002; N. Ellis 1996, 2003); word-knowledge dimensions (Nation 2001; Schmitt 2008); listening load (Field 2008; Vandergrift & Goh 2012); YL sources (Cameron 2001; Pinter 2017).

## Procedure
Set item counts by age; teach as chunks ("I like football", "on the table"); keep listening-heavy lessons light; teach transparent cognates in bigger batches; recycle across modalities.

## Limits & critique
- The numbers are his synthesis. Don't cite them as findings.
- "Extra items don't increase retention" is unsourced.
- Partly experience-based (18 years with ages 7–10).
- **Transfer caveat:** written for UK secondary MFL (French/Spanish/German) with ~2–3 lessons a week and minimal out-of-class exposure. Tashkent EFL learners get far more ambient English (YouTube, games), so input-scarcity arguments apply less strongly. *(Claude)*

## Apply in my context *(Claude)*
- Audit Kid's Box / Prepare pages: a typical YL coursebook page introduces 8–12 items. By Conti's targets that's 2–3 lessons' worth. So split the page, don't rush it.
- The Cambridge YL wordlists are manageable at 3–4 items per lesson *if* every item comes back repeatedly. What makes the load unrealistic is low recycling, not list length.
