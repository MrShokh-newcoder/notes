> Stories are central to identity and comprehension. They give children a shared context for participating, natural language and real issues to think about.

## Claim and examples
- A *Meg and Mog* drama project with 6–7-year-old beginners: a week of class shows with masks, props and programmes. Afterwards the children wanted to act out every story.
- *Something Else* (Kathryn Cave) told to 30 children aged 9–12 in a post-war country, with 90 teachers observing. Read was struck by how mature the children's thinking was.

## Theory
Bruner (1996, *The Culture of Education*): "narrative sensibility"; "we represent our lives to ourselves … in the form of narrative." Wells (2009, *The Meaning Makers*): Bristol study, early story exposure predicts later literacy (L1).

## Limits
Anecdotal; argues from L1 research by analogy. No step-by-step storytelling method in the post.

## Apply in my context *(Claude)*
- Choose picturebooks whose content is culturally comfortable for your learners. *Something Else* (belonging and difference) works well and raises nothing sensitive.
- Story → drama → performance gives the Starters/Movers storytelling task (Speaking: picture story) a meaningful rehearsal context.
