> Contra Walter (2012), chunks aren't dead-end memorisation. Semi-fixed chunks with slots are templates from which grammar is analysed out ('bootstrapping'), so chunk teaching and explicit grammar can coexist.

## Claim
Replies to Catherine Walter, "Time to stop avoiding grammar" (*Guardian Weekly*, 18 Sep 2012). "Learning chunks actually facilitates the teaching of grammar and serves as a basis for mastery of the grammar system."

## Theory
- N. Ellis (1996) "Sequencing in SLA: phonological memory, chunking, and points of order", *SSLA* 18(1): learners memorise chunks, then analyse them.
- R. Ellis (2005) "Principles of instructed language learning", *Asian EFL Journal* 7: formulaic chunks first, rules later.
- Comments: Wray (2002); Skehan's analytic vs holistic learners.

## Procedure
1. Teach a semi-fixed chunk with a slot: *Could you pass me the ___, please?* → water / menu / my phone.
2. Use chunks as templates for new utterances: *If it doesn't work out, you can always ___.*
3. At low levels, formulaic language first (*How are you? I don't know.*), rules later.

## Limits
The "50–80% of native speech is formulaic" figure is unsourced in the post (Erman & Warren 2000 is the usual source, ~55%) *(Claude)*.

## Apply in my context *(Claude)*
- This is the theoretical bridge between YL (all chunks: songs, routines) and teens (rules). Starters–Flyers routines *are* the chunk bank that KET grammar later analyses.
